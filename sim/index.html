    About this reconstruction

    A reconstruction of the Chicxulub impact 66 million years ago: the target, the crater forming, the sea's return, the tsunami and the global aftermath, from three hours before the impact to 100,000 years after. One model reaches from the crater out to the whole Earth.

    How to read it

    The time column on the right runs upward. Its scale changes where it is marked: hours before the impact, seconds and minutes around it, hours after it, and from two days on each step is ten times longer than the last. Drag it, click a stage name, or press Play.

    Close up, the crater is a block 360 km across that reaches 40 km down into the mantle; choose a cut in Options to look inside. Each rock keeps the colour of the layer it started in, so the layers can be followed through the deformation. Dashed rings mark where the crater rim (180–200 km across) and the peak ring will form. Around the Gulf, the solid line is the shore 66 million years ago; the dashed lines are today's coasts, moved to where their rocks were then. Relief is at true scale; 3× and 6× are in Options.

    Scroll out and the Gulf curves away into the whole Earth as it was then. Click anywhere on the globe to see what reached that place, and when. From two days on, a panel follows the impact winter. The dust, smoke and haze hide much of the ground for days and years, as they would have; to look underneath, turn off Atmosphere in Options, which draws the ground as if the air were clear and the sunlight undimmed.

    Season and light

    The impact is set in boreal spring (austral autumn): growth bands and isotopes in the bones of fish killed within the first hour, at Tanis in North Dakota, show they died as their spring growth was speeding up (During et al. 2022). The time of day is not known. Every place is lit as the crater region is, by a sun 38° up in the south-east, rather than by one sun with a fixed night side, since the Earth turns under the Sun through the days and years shown. The climate model works with annual means, so the season only sets the scene: the authors suggest it mattered for survival, with northern life in its spring growth and southern life heading into winter dormancy.

    The target

    Constrained: seismic surveys show a carbonate ramp that deepened from tens of metres of water in the south-southwest to about 2 km in the north-northeast, where the Mesozoic layers are also thickest (Gulick et al. 2008). Models use about 3 km of sediments under 2 km of water, and 4 km of sediments, as end-members for the north-east (Collins et al. 2008), on crust about 33 km thick (Collins et al. 2020). Boreholes found limestone, dolomite and thick anhydrite in the Cretaceous section, and granite in the deep crust.

    Modelled: the ramp's exact shape and its gently curved depth contours, the split of the pile into four layers, the bedding and the boundary inside the crust. Small-scale roughness is generated.

    The impact

    A kinematic sketch of the numerical models of Morgan et al. (2016) and Collins et al. (2020), not a simulation of its own. A smooth displacement field moves the rock: a cavity about 100 km wide and 30 km deep after one minute, a central uplift more than 10 km high at three minutes, and its outward collapse into the peak ring, with terraced walls, by ten minutes. Ejecta near the crater are ballistic particles launched as the cavity grows. The asteroid (about 12 km across, about 20 km/s, entering at 60° from the north-east) uses representative values; it is drawn dark, as a carbonaceous asteroid (Fischer-Gödde et al. 2024). Its glow in the air follows the air density, rising some 20,000-fold over its last 100 km. It is drawn where it really is: far out as a point of reflected sunlight (bright as a first-magnitude star three hours out), then as a dark body once it spans a few pixels.

    The sea and the tsunami

    After ten minutes the sea is computed. A shallow-water model (pipeline/swe.py: friction rougher on land than at sea, wetting and drying, a simple breaking limit) runs from 10 minutes to 10 hours over the Gulf on a 2.4 km grid. It starts from the crater as it stands at 10 minutes and from a rim wave tuned so the open Gulf sees crests of about 100 m, up to about 300 m (Range et al. 2022). The resurge into the crater, the breaking on the shelves and the flooding of the coastal plains come out of that model. It has no wave dispersion and uses coarse paleo-topography, so flood limits are tens of kilometres uncertain; the first 10 minutes are a sketch. A second run of the same physics on a 0.25° global grid (pipeline/globe.py) follows the tsunami around the world for 48 hours; it does not flood land. Checked against the global simulations of Range et al. (2022): open-water crests in the Gulf of over 100 m (here 131 m median), entry into the Pacific through the Central American Seaway after about 4 hours (here 4.7), the Indian Ocean reached by about a day (here 22 hours), over 10 m offshore along many North Atlantic coasts (here 59% of them) and some South American Pacific coasts (21%), and the Tethys, Indian Ocean, South Atlantic and North Pacific largely sheltered (median crests 1–2 m).

    The Gulf and the Earth are the 65 Ma PALEOMAP paleogeography (Scotese and Wright 2018), with plate rotations from the GPlates Web Service. PALEOMAP draws parts of Yucatán as low land; near the crater they are treated as the shallow carbonate shelf the target shows. The heights are PALEOMAP's highest-resolution version, 0.1° cells (about 11 km), drawn at that resolution on the globe (the relief shading comes from it, finer than the globe's mesh) and on the Gulf grid; its heights come in 40 m steps, and sea level is put midway between its 0 m sea and 40 m land cells. The tsunami runs use the same map (the global one averaged to 0.25°). The coastlines are drawn from a smooth fit to it on finer grids (610 m over the Gulf, 0.06° elsewhere, pipeline/coast.py), with small wiggles that move the shore by at most 1.5 km in the Gulf and 6 km elsewhere. Those wiggles are illustrative, not known.

    Colours are true colour, as a satellite would see them on a cloud-free day (pipeline/landcover.py). The land cover is a rule, not a vegetation model: rainfall from latitude belts, raised near the sea and on high ground, and temperature from latitude and height, following reconstructions of latest Cretaceous vegetation (Upchurch, Otto-Bliesner and Scotese 1999): a mostly forested greenhouse world with rainforest in the tropics, deciduous forests reaching the poles, scrub and deserts in the subtropical interiors, rock on the highest ground and no ice sheets. There were no grasslands yet, so open country is scrub and bare ground. Where its boundaries fall is illustrative. Water is coloured by how deep the light goes before it returns (clear seawater takes red within a few metres), so shallow shelves are turquoise and deep ocean dark blue.

    The stars are today's real sky: the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (about 8,400 stars to magnitude 6.5, coloured by their measured B−V) over NASA's Deep Star Maps 2020 Milky Way from Gaia (NASA/Goddard Space Flight Center Scientific Visualization Studio; Gaia DR2: ESA/Gaia/DPAC), with the celestial pole on the 66 Ma Earth's axis. The time of day is not known, so the sky is held still with right ascension 0h over the map's 0° meridian. At 66 Ma it would not have looked like this: in 66 million years every naked-eye star has moved hundreds of light-years, and many of today's brightest, Orion's among them, had not yet formed; only the band of the Milky Way would have looked broadly the same.

    Re-entry, air blast and earthquakes

    Ejecta re-entry (pipeline/reentry.py): two million particles leave the crater above the atmosphere at 2–11 km/s, fly orbits over the turning Earth and fall back in; their mass is set so the distal layer averages 3 mm. The cloud they form is drawn too: points from the same launch law leave the crater and fly the same orbits, glowing for the first minute as the vapour plume condenses into molten spherules, then a dusty veil that fans out over the continents (most of it to the south-west, away from where the asteroid came in), rises thousands of kilometres and thins as it falls back over the first hours. Each point stands for an equal share of the mass, so most of it falls within a few hundred kilometres in minutes; the faster tenth carries on around the globe. Far from the crater the upper atmosphere takes some 20–90 kW/m² for about an hour, 15–65 times sunlight, below the 50–150 times Melosh et al. (1990) estimated. How much reached the ground is debated: the falling spherules shield the surface, and Goldin and Melosh (2009) find about 6 kW/m² at peak for some 25 minutes; the probe gives both.

    Air blast and shaking follow the Earth Impact Effects Program (Collins et al. 2005): a magnitude near 10, overpressure scaled from surface explosions, shaking intensity from Californian ground-motion data. The blast formula is least reliable far away; there the pressure pulse travels as a Lamb wave at about 318 m/s, the speed measured for the 2022 Hunga Tonga eruption, and circles the globe. Between about 103° and 142° from the crater no direct P or S waves arrive (the core shadow); beyond it, only P waves refracted through the core.

    Wildfires

    The heat of the re-entering debris can set vegetation alight, but how much reached the ground is debated, and so is the evidence. Charcoal in the crater's first-day deposits shows that nearby land burned (Gulick et al. 2019), and the boundary layer worldwide holds soot (Wolbach et al. 1988); yet North American boundary rocks hold four to eight times less charcoal than the rocks below them (Belcher et al. 2005), and part of the soot may come from oil and coal in the crater's own rocks (Lyons et al. 2020). In experiments, impact-like heat ignites dry litter readily but live forest only under a long pulse (Belcher et al. 2015).

    Here, each place catches fire when the heat at the ground passes 12 kW/m² (about nine times sunlight) for 4 minutes (litter) or 20 minutes (live forest); near the crater the heat is intense but lasts only about a quarter of an hour, so there, as in the experiments, litter burns but forest does not, with fuel patchy and fires spreading over hours from scattered ignition points as thin, ragged fronts, each drawn out downwind into an ellipse (Van Wagner 1969; Alexander 1985): the head fire runs at about 1.5 km/h in a moderate breeze, up to about 4 km/h in the strongest, with the tallest flames, while the back creeps tens to hundreds of metres an hour; smoke trails downwind, and the fires leave charred, ash-flecked ground that ferns retake within about a decade once the light returns (the fern spike in the pollen record; tsunami mud on flooded coasts is overgrown about as fast). The winds are the prevailing ones of early May at 66 Ma, from a climate model run on the same map (HadCM3L, Valdes, Scotese and Lunt 2021): north-east and south-east trades in the tropics, westerlies beyond, the surface wind for the spread and the wind 1-3 km up for the smoke. They are monthly means, steady in the trade-wind belts but in middle latitudes the average of changeable weather, so there the smoke wanders more about them; what the impact did to the winds is not included. Patchy assumes the falling spherules shield the ground, so it gets a tenth of the upper-air heating (Goldin and Melosh 2009): litter fires in scattered patches across about a third of vegetated land, almost no forest fires. Firestorm assumes no shielding, half of the heating (Melosh et al. 1990): litter catches on nearly all vegetated land and forest on about half, as the soot scenario of the impact winter requires.

    Impact winter and the long warm-up

    A small energy-balance model (pipeline/climate.py): land, an ocean surface layer that overturns into the deep ocean as it chills, heat carried between latitudes, and Maastrichtian annual means as the baseline. The darkness takes away each day's own sunlight for the latitude and season, starting in mid boreal spring, so the north loses its first summer's sunshine while the south heads into winter with little to lose; northern land cools fastest in the first months, the south catches up in its own summer. Temperatures are shown as departures from the normal for the time of year, and from about 2 years on (when the frames are too far apart to follow the seasons) as running annual means. The haze spreads from where the impact put it and is mixed around the globe by stratospheric winds. Studies disagree about what darkened the sky, so there are three scenarios, each tuned to its study's headline numbers:

    • Fine dust (Senel et al. 2023): here about 1.6 years of darkness and −19 °C at the coldest; published, almost 2 years and up to −15 °C.
    • Soot from global fires (Bardeen et al. 2017; Tabor et al. 2020): 1.9 years under 1% of normal light; land −29, ocean −11, global −16 °C; published, about 2 years and −28, −11, −16.5 °C.
    • Sulfate (Brugger et al. 2017): −26 °C, a global mean below freezing for about 7 years, more than 30 years to recover; published, at least −26 °C, 3–16 years, over 30 years.

    After the cold comes about 5 °C of warming lasting some 100,000 years, recorded in fish-tooth oxygen isotopes at El Kef, Tunisia (MacLeod et al. 2018). It is drawn as recorded, stronger toward the poles, not modelled.

    Fossil sites and boundary rocks

    Seventeen places where the end of the Cretaceous can be seen in the rocks are marked where those rocks stood at the time: Tanis and Hell Creek with the last dinosaurs, the boundary sections at Gubbio, Stevns Klint, Caravaca and El Kef where the iridium layer was found and the boundary is defined, the spherule beds and tsunami deposits around the Gulf, drill cores from the Atlantic, and sites as far off as Antarctica, New Zealand and India. Diamonds are boundary sections, circles fossil sites. Today's positions (rounded to about 0.1°, since some excavations publish no exact spot) are moved to 66 Ma with the PALEOMAP plate model, the same rotations that place the crater and the Gulf's towns. Click one to read what its rocks show next to what the models give for the same place: when the waves and the spherules arrived, how hot the sky got. At nine of the twelve marine sites the 66 Ma map shows land, often only metres high, where the rocks show sea floor: its coastlines are approximate at this scale, and each card says so.

    How the views fit together

    The regional map is centred on the crater and keeps true distances and directions from it, so it wraps exactly onto the globe. The crater block shows its own detailed model inside a disc 344 km across; the Gulf grid (2.4 km cells) hands over to the global data (0.25°) across a band about 220 km wide near its edge, where the two tsunami runs also meet.

    Not shown

    The seasonal cycle and the day–night cycle, weather and rainfall, ozone loss, acid rain, smoke plumes as separate clouds, and life.
